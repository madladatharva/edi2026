# Literature Review: Urban Flood Nowcasting

## 1. Rainfall-Runoff Modeling Fundamentals
Urban flood prediction relies fundamentally on understanding rainfall-runoff behavior. In a natural catchment, vegetation and soil absorb a significant portion of precipitation, slowing the flow of water into channels. However, rapid urbanization replaces these natural surfaces with impermeable concrete and asphalt. This drastically alters the hydrological response: peak runoff occurs much faster, and the total volume of surface water increases significantly. 
The core parameters determining this response are rainfall intensity, duration, and the localized land cover. When high-intensity rainfall persists even for a short duration (a "burst"), the precipitation rate quickly exceeds the infiltration capacity of any remaining soil and the designed flow rates of artificial drainage. Our prototype focuses on exactly this dynamic: taking immediate rainfall intensity and translating it into a rapid accumulation penalty, recognizing that the "runoff" in a heavily concretized urban ward is nearly instantaneous.

## 2. DEM-based Flood Susceptibility Mapping
In modern hydrological engineering, Digital Elevation Models (DEM) are crucial. A high-resolution DEM (such as those generated via LiDAR or ISRO's CartoDEM) provides a pixel-by-pixel topographic map of an area. Professional flood susceptibility models use this data to calculate slope, flow direction, and flow accumulation to predict exactly where water will pool (sinks).
For this pilot project, ingesting and processing gigabytes of raw DEM raster data in real-time is beyond the computational scope of a student prototype. Instead, we have abstracted this concept. Our synthetic dataset provides a base `elevation_m` field for each localized zone. Our risk model acts as a simplified topological evaluation: it penalizes zones with lower absolute elevation relative to the ward's average height, mimicking how water naturally gravitates toward and pools in topographic sinks.

## 3. EPA SWMM vs. Prototype Risk Modeling
The US Environmental Protection Agency's Storm Water Management Model (EPA SWMM) is the industry standard for modeling urban runoff and drainage networks. SWMM is a dynamic rainfall-runoff simulation model used for single-event or long-term simulation. It requires exhaustive parameterization, including precise pipe diameters, invert elevations, manhole depths, and complex St. Venant hydrodynamic equations for routing water through closed conduits.
While highly accurate, SWMM requires months of calibration and is computationally heavy, making it difficult to use for sub-minute "nowcasting" without substantial infrastructure. Therefore, our `risk_model.py` is a deliberate, necessary simplification. Rather than simulating hydraulic head pressure or dynamic wave routing, we use a deterministic penalty system. We collapse the complex capacity of an area's stormwater network into a single `drainage_capacity_score` (1-5). This allows our prototype to achieve the primary goal—sub-5-second risk visualization—while proving the conceptual logic that restricted drainage exponentially worsens topographical risk during a rain event.

## 4. Real-World Precedents in Urban Flood Warning
Several global cities have implemented short-term nowcasting systems to mitigate urban flood damage. For example, cities in Japan and early-warning grids in parts of Europe utilize localized X-band radar networks integrated with municipal telemetry (IoT water level sensors in drains). These systems detect intense micro-cell storms and issue automated warnings to specific neighborhoods before the water actually pools on the streets, allowing residents to move vehicles or deploy barriers. Such systems prioritize speed and geographic precision over long-term meteorological forecasting.

## 5. Addressing the Gap
This prototype directly addresses the gap identified in our Problem Statement: the lack of hyper-local, actionable nowcasting in Indian municipalities like Pune. While state-level forecasts are too broad ("heavy rain tomorrow"), our system demonstrates that by integrating micro-elevation and local drainage constraints with real-time rainfall data, a city can pinpoint exact sub-ward intersections that are about to flood, enabling highly targeted disaster response.
